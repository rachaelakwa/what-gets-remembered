# What Gets Remembered?

**What Gets Remembered?** is a digital archive created for **Session 10 of the Digital Humanities Tutorial**, led by **Rachael Akwa** on **November 12, 2026**.

The session, **“What Gets Remembered: Digital Archives, Power, and Interactivity,”** asks:

> **What has to happen for something to become part of the digital record?**

The site was built from field notes and a transcript from an October 2026 visit to the Internet Archive in San Francisco. It turns selected moments from that visit into a small interactive archive that students can explore before and during the session.

Rather than treating digital preservation as simply saving a file, the project examines how preservation is shaped by people, institutions, technologies, infrastructure, legal and political contexts, and differences in power over what can be created, changed, preserved, restricted, discovered, or accessed.

## About the Archive

This archive is a selective reconstruction of one visit to the Internet Archive.

It is not intended to provide a complete account of the institution. Instead, it turns a small set of observations from the visit into records that can be encountered through different pathways.

The collection currently contains nine records:

1. The Wayback Machine at Scale
2. Government Websites at Transition
3. The Book Scanner
4. Prince of Persia in a Browser
5. Television News Archive
6. Making the Web Browsable
7. Copies in Multiple Places
8. Different Kinds of Access
9. Climate GPT and Public AI

The site also contains shorter fragments from the visit that are intentionally left open to interpretation.

## Ways to Explore

Visitors can enter the archive through:

- Search
- Browse by Material
- Browse by Question
- Random Record
- Related Records
- A browsing trail of previously opened records

These pathways are part of the project itself. Different interfaces can make different records, relationships, and ideas easier or harder to notice.

## Why the Interface Matters

The interface is not only a way to access the archive. It is also part of what students are asked to examine.

The same nine records can be encountered through search, categories, questions, random discovery, and links between related records.

The project therefore asks not only:

> **What gets preserved?**

but also:

> **How does the way an archive is organized shape what people encounter and understand?**

## Course Context

This project was created as part of the **Digital Humanities Tutorial** at Minerva University.

During Session 10, students use the archive as a case study to examine:

- the actors and forces that shape digital preservation and access
- differences in power across archival systems
- how interfaces shape discovery and interpretation
- how similar choices appear in students’ own city microarchives
